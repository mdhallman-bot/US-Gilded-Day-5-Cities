1 · Thinking is Fun!

You discover a new band on Spotify and love its music. You listen for a month. Then you learn that the songs and the band were created using AI. Does that discovery change how you feel about the music? Why or why not?

Write a justified initial answer in 2–3 sentences. This opening is ungraded. Then open the reveal and think through the follow-ups; no additional writing is required.

Sentence frames · optional

My feelings would/would not change because ____. What matters most to me is ____.

Reveal perspectives and deeper follow-ups

The listening experience: if the sound and your enjoyment have not changed, the music may still have value.

Human expression: you may care that a person experienced something and chose how to express it. The story behind a song can be part of what you value.

Honesty and credit: you might enjoy the song but object to a fictional band being presented as real musicians, or care about whose work contributed to it. These are separate judgments.

  1. If the music sounds exactly the same, what changed when you learned its origin?
  2. Would it matter whether a person wrote the lyrics and used AI for the instruments? Where would you draw the line?
  3. Would you feel differently if the artist disclosed the AI use from the beginning? Can you enjoy a song and still object to how it was made?
  4. If you have a limited music budget, would you pay the same amount to hear AI-generated music as human musicians? What are you paying for?

Final twist: what if this song helped you through a difficult experience before you learned how it was made? Does its origin change what that experience meant to you?

2 · Meet Lucia Russo

Sixteen years old. A new city. A notebook from home.

Lucia was born in 1885 in a village near Salerno in southern Italy. Her father, Antonio, works on other people’s farms and takes building jobs when available. Her mother, Rosa, sews and manages the household. Her brother, Paolo, is nine.

Lucia attended village school for several years. She likes reading, sketches people in the margins, and misses her friend Teresa. Her dream is to earn money and keep learning. She sometimes disagrees with her parents about what her future should be.

Her father’s brother Carlo moved to New York in 1897. His letters and help with passage make migration possible. The family follows him in spring 1901. Lucia’s diary is imagined in Italian and presented here in English; she is still learning English.

Our investigation: Why did people move, what did they encounter, and how did city politics gain power over their opportunities?

Lucia, her family, dates, conversations, and diary are invented. Historical sources investigate the setting; they do not prove these events happened to this family. Their experience represents one possible path, not all immigrants.

Learning goal: Explain how immigration and urban growth created opportunities and needs, and use sources to evaluate political-machine support and corruption.

3 · The decision to leave

Lucia’s diary · Historical fiction · February 1901 · Near Salerno

A letter on the table

Dear Diary,

Carlo’s letter arrived with money inside. Papa read the part about work twice. Here he waits for someone to need another pair of hands. Mama stretches what we earn, but there is never much left when the work stops.

Carlo says New York has jobs and shops where people speak Italian. He has found a room we could share until we settle. He did not say the streets were made of gold. He said a person could earn.

I want to see it. Then I think of Teresa and Nonna, and I want everyone to stop talking about the ship. Mama folded the letter and asked, “What will we do if the work there stops too?”

Investigate the decision

Many Italians left amid rural poverty and limited economic opportunities. Family letters, money, and connections helped others follow. Some migrants intended to earn money and return; migration was not always a one-way decision. People from other places also moved for reasons including persecution and political insecurity.

Push factor: a condition encouraging departure. Pull factor: an attraction of a destination. The same decision can involve both.

Sentence startersOne pressure to leave is ____. One attraction is ____. A cost beyond money is ____.

4 · Arrival is a beginning

Lucia’s diary · Historical fiction · April 1901 · New York

So many voices

Dear Diary,

On the ship I practiced the English words Carlo sent. When someone finally spoke to me, the words tangled together. Papa watched every official. Mama kept Paolo close. I was afraid we might be separated.

Carlo was waiting when we reached the city. He carried a bag and talked so quickly that Mama laughed for the first time in days.

On his street I heard familiar voices. A woman asked which village we came from. There were shops, wagons, children, and more people than I could count. I had imagined feeling alone in America. That evening, at least, I did not.

Analyze from your seat: Identify the island, the harbor, and Manhattan. How might being close to the city, but separated by water, affect a newcomer’s arrival? Separate what you can see from what you infer.

Ellis Island opened in 1892 and processed many immigrants arriving through New York. The main building shown here opened in December 1900, before Lucia’s imagined April 1901 arrival. Inspection and possible detention made arrival uncertain. Relatives, neighbors, religious institutions, and mutual-aid organizations helped newcomers find information and support. They did not depend only on politicians.

Not everyone faced the same door

Nativism and discrimination limited belonging and opportunity. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 barred Chinese laborers from immigrating, with exceptions; restrictions were extended. Italian migration cannot stand in for Chinese, Jewish, Mexican, or other experiences.

Belonging was contested

The Library of Congress describes hostility toward Italian immigrants, including stereotypes portraying them as inferior or threatening. Nativism favors people considered native-born over newcomers. Discrimination turns an attitude into unequal treatment, such as denying a person a job because of their background. This is historical background, not a quotation from Lucia.

The Chinese Exclusion Act restricted Chinese laborers; it did not ban Lucia’s Italian family. Different groups faced different legal restrictions and prejudices. Do not assume all immigrant experiences were alike.

5 · A neighborhood worth living in

Lucia’s diary · Historical fiction · May 1901

A place at the window

Dear Diary,

The room is smaller than Carlo’s letters made it seem. I sleep close enough to Paolo to hear him dream. But from the window I can watch the whole street. I have drawn the fruit seller three times. He never stands still.

Paolo keeps asking about school. I want to keep learning too. Mama says my sewing helps pay for our room; she also asks Carlo where I could learn more English. I wish a day had room for everything.

Mama has found sewing through a neighbor. Papa leaves early for laboring work. On Sunday we visited people Carlo knows, and Mama exchanged news of home.

I like being able to buy something without walking to the next village. I like that there is always someone outside. Papa says all these people mean customers and work. Mama says they also mean everyone looking for the same rooms.

Industry and commerce drew migrants from abroad and from within the United States. Cities offered work, customers, transportation, entertainment, and community. Rapid growth also put pressure on housing and public services.

Sentence frames · optional

I notice ____. This suggests an opportunity/challenge because ____. The image cannot establish ____.

School, work, and the future

Public schools offered immigrant children opportunities to learn English and build new connections. School could also bring pressure to change names or customs. Children’s experiences varied by age, language, school, and family circumstances.

Wages and work at home could be important to a household’s survival. School time and study time could therefore compete with paid work, sewing, caring for siblings, or other responsibilities. That tension does not mean immigrant parents did not value education. Lucia’s exact choices are fictional; the competing demands are historically grounded.

Neighborhood shops, skilled trades, religious institutions, relatives, and mutual-aid groups could also create opportunity. Earning money, getting an education, and belonging to a community were connected—but access was uneven.

Historical context: Library of Congress, A Century in the Spotlight and Working Across the Country.

6 · Investigate two sides of city life

Lucia’s diary · Historical fiction · June 1901

Windows and breathing room

Dear Diary,

The heat stays in the room after the sun goes down. Mama opens the window, but the air hardly moves. We must be careful about water and keep the stairway clear. There are so many families using so little space.

Paolo wants somewhere to play that is not the street. Yesterday a neighbor told us about a playground. He came home describing it as if he had discovered a country.

I miss the space outside our village. I do not miss wondering whether Papa will find work. Must we choose between one and the other?

After observing: photographer and context

Riis used photographs to encourage reform. The pictured Hell’s Kitchen building lacked interior windows, ventilation, and indoor plumbing. The playground was a privately funded experiment. These selected places do not show every New Yorker’s life. Riis’s choices and descriptions also reflected his own assumptions and prejudices.

Problems included crowded housing, poor sanitation, fire risk, and limited light and air. Communities, charities, and public officials pursued improvements. Neither hardship nor improvement was evenly distributed.

LOC, Riis and Reform.

Sanitation: whose responsibility?

Sanitation includes clean-water systems, toilets and sewers, garbage collection, and street cleaning. In crowded districts, accumulated waste and poorly maintained toilets could contaminate surroundings and increase disease risks. Some tenements relied on shared facilities rather than a toilet in every apartment. Conditions differed from building to building.

One careful family could wash and dispose of its waste properly yet still be affected by unsafe shared facilities or uncollected refuse. Landlords, residents, and city officials controlled different parts of the problem. A public service could reach people across many households.

Improvements were already underway before Lucia arrived. The Library of Congress describes the city’s uniformed street-cleaning force, the “White Wings,” associated with reforms begun under George Waring in 1895. Better services were possible, but that did not mean every housing or sanitation problem was solved.

Sources: LOC, Riis and Reform and LOC, White Wings on review.

8 · How does help become power?

A political machine is an organization that builds and maintains political power through a network of leaders, workers, and supporters.

Tammany Hall was associated with New York City’s Democratic Party. Local organizers built relationships, helped residents navigate city life, and sought political loyalty. Some offered emergency assistance and access to jobs. The help could be valuable while also strengthening the organization.

Assistance and connections→Loyalty and campaign work→Support from eligible voters→Influence over jobs and decisions

Lucia cannot vote. A newly arrived father could not simply vote immediately either: citizenship and eligibility mattered. Organizers could seek family and neighborhood support, help eligible men pursue naturalization, and build future loyalty.

A machine politician explains himself · 1905

George Washington Plunkitt was a Tammany district leader. Journalist William L. Riordon recorded his talks. Read his language as a politician defending his own work, not a neutral report:

“If there’s a family in my district in want I know it before the charitable societies do, and me and my men are first on the ground. … The consequence is that the poor look up to George W. Plunkitt as a father, come to him in trouble—and don’t forget him on election day.”

Plunkitt of Tammany Hall (1905), chapter 6. Original public-domain text; omission marked. Read source. “In want” means lacking necessities.

Sentence frames · optional

Distinguish the practices

Patronage rewards supporters. Graft enriches insiders. Kickbacks, bribery, and election fraud are different from ordinary assistance or lawful persuasion. Not every person helped was bribed, and not every supporter was deceived.

Hypothetical example—not a historical bill: A contractor charges the city $10,000 for $6,000 of work and secretly returns $2,000 to an official who steered the contract. Taxpayers pay the inflated bill; the official gains privately.

A name in an old newspaper

Dear Diary,

Papa has work again. The club’s man spoke to someone, and then there was a place for him. Papa comes home tired, but he comes home talking.
